# Point
To say how pages here are shaped, and how to choose which assumptions a page states, so that the reasoning behind every decision is recorded somewhere and can be re-examined later.

# Assumptions
**Values**
- Re-examining our reasoning is part of following the creed.
- Be upfront: a reader should know what was assumed before reading what was made.
- Recording what we can is valuable, even though the record will be incomplete.

**Relies on**
- [The Creed](../creed/creed.md) (not yet written)

**Premises**
- Reasoning kept apart from the thing it justifies tends to drift from it.
- Others may read these pages one day, as participants rather than an audience.
- Stoa's documentation is a reasonable starting point, though not to be copied exactly.

# The piece

## The graph
Pages form a general graph. Each page is a node. An edge means "this page relies on that page's conclusions", and is listed under **Relies on**. Folders are only for finding things.

Every decision has its reasoning somewhere, but not every page must carry it: a page may link to the page that reasons for it.

## The shape of a page
Metadata (title, status, date), then:
1. **Point**: what the page is for.
2. **Assumptions**: where the page starts (see below).
3. **The piece**: the thing being made. It includes justification only if we decide its readers deserve it there.
4. **Unresolved**: open questions, placed before the justification so readers can ponder them first.
5. **Justification**: why it was made this way.

Exceptions:
- **The creed** stands bare, as the starting point. Its reasoning goes on another page.
- **Reflections** keep the assumptions at the front, since listeners should know what the speaker assumed. They may be in the speaker's own voice.

## Choosing which assumptions to state
An assumptions section is where the page *chooses to start*. It has three parts:
- **Values**: the goals the page serves. These are not true or false.
- **Relies on**: conclusions of other pages.
- **Premises**: what the page takes as given about the world.

For each candidate, ask:
1. **Would the piece change, or not exist, without this?** If not, leave it out.
2. **Am I going to argue for this here, or start from it?** If argued, it goes in the justification. If started from, it goes here.

Any assumption can be argued for one level deeper. Stop at something another page argues, something the intended reader would grant, or the creed, which is taken on faith.

Reasoning is mostly inductive, so each step is also being assumed valid. Steps stay in the justification; steps we doubt are named in Unresolved.

## Limitations we accept
We cannot see all our assumptions, put every reason into words, or notice every open question. We record what we can and continue.

# Unresolved
- The method has not been tested. Choosing what is essential, and where to stop arguing, takes judgment.
- The lines between values, premises, inferences and conclusions are not clean.
- If a premise is listed because the reader would grant it, it may be the one most in need of questioning.
- Is the overhead worth it, or will it discourage writing?
- Is "one idea per page" a rule here?
- Which status words fit a church that keeps re-examining itself? "Provisional" is a placeholder.

# Justification
**Reasoning is kept** so it can be re-examined, as authenticity asks.

**Assumptions come early** because being upfront matters more than a smooth start. For sermons we considered that caveats might weaken the speaker. But that assumes a speaker who claims authority, which this church does not give.

**The piece comes before its justification** so it can be used without reading the argument, while keeping the two together limits drift.

**The choosing method went through a rejected version.** It asked "is this argued for on this page?", which a writer cannot answer before writing the page. It also asked "if this were false, would the piece change?", which does not fit values. The current questions replace these.

**Stopping is a choice** because no chain of reasons has a natural bottom. Looking for the most fundamental assumption would never end.
